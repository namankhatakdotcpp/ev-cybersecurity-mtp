The first analysis step was a read-only audit of the repository and the data (Gate 0). Its central finding is that the original "risky decision" label is largely associated with how many kiosks were attacked at the station, which is randomised by the layout and logged before the participant decides.

| Attacked kiosks at the station | Share of decisions labelled risky |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0% (192 of 1,123 rows, 17.1%; risky is impossible) |
| 1 | 26.5% |
| 2 | 61.5% |

The number of attacked kiosks alone separates risky from non-risky decisions with a descriptive AUROC of 0.767 over all rows and 0.827 among charge decisions (Gate 0 audit, section 0). Participants did not avoid attacked kiosks: 48.3% of charge decisions were at an attacked kiosk, against roughly 50.9% expected if an available kiosk had been chosen at random (approximate).

The earlier behaviour-only AUROC of 0.666 [0.632, 0.700] (behaviour plus gaze 0.642 [0.605, 0.679]) is largely a skip-versus-charge detector: the label requires a charge, so all 315 skips are non-risky, and a score that knew only whether the participant charged would reach an AUROC of 0.715. Window duration has a pooled descriptive AUROC of 0.664 but 0.488 among charge decisions, which is chance. Among the 777 charge rows with gaze, the six gaze features have descriptive per-feature AUROCs of 0.49 to 0.52 for the original label.

**The target was therefore redefined after the data had been seen.** The primary target is whether the chosen kiosk is attacked, given a charge at a station that has at least one attacked and at least one safe available kiosk, with the share of attacked kiosks entering as an exposure offset. The primary set has 492 rows from 112 participants and 220 events (chosen kiosk attacked). The redefinition was not blind: Gate 0 looked at the same rows that the later gates reuse, and no untouched test set exists.

Skipping a station is not associated with exposure in the way an informed response would be: across the 7 cells of attacked-by-available kiosks the skip rate ranges from 0.221 to 0.329, with no rise at stations where nothing is attacked or fall at stations where everything is attacked, and the intervals overlap. ## 2. Primary hypothesis H1: does gaze add to behaviour and exposure?

H1 was fixed in Protocol v1 before the analysis and has two parts, both of which must hold: (A) a likelihood-ratio test of the six gaze features in a random-intercept logistic model that already contains the exposure offset, the design variables and the behaviour variables, and (B) a participant-grouped out-of-sample comparison of behaviour plus gaze against behaviour alone. Methods: marginal maximum likelihood with Gauss-Hermite quadrature [CITE: mixed-effects logistic regression], repeated nested participant-grouped cross-validation, participant-level bootstrap [CITE: cluster bootstrap], block permutation of gaze across participants.

- Claim A: LRT chi-square 8.17 on 6 df, p = 0.226; participant-block permutation p = 0.387 (run `h1_claim_a`).
- Claim B: mean out-of-fold AUROC 0.626 [0.569, 0.679] for behaviour and 0.631 [0.574, 0.684] for behaviour plus gaze; difference +0.005 [-0.006, +0.015]; permuted-gaze control p = 0.493 (run `h1_claim_b`).
- By the rule in the protocol, H1 is supported only if both claims hold. Neither does: **H1 is not supported.**

## 6. Limitations

- **No untouched test set.** Earlier analysis rounds (D045 to D063) iterated on the same participants, and the later gates reuse the same rows. Every result here is pre-specified but exploratory, and a confirmatory claim would need new data or a pre-registered analysis.
- **The target was redefined after the data had been seen** (Gate 0), so the redefinition was not blind. The choice was motivated by the audit finding that the original label follows exposure.
- **The best predictive model is the exposure-only reference.** No behaviour, gaze or EEG feature set was associated with better out-of-sample discrimination than exposure and design alone, so the benchmark does not support any learned detector.
- **Power.** The primary set has a few hundred rows and a couple of hundred events; the EEG subset has about a third of the rows. Intervals around AUROC differences are wide, so the results are "not detected", which is different from "no effect".
- **EEG selection and quality.** The matched subset favours early stations and a particular collection period; the headset has four channels, minimal preprocessing was applied, and the window is defined by the decision event.
- **The decision window ends at the choice.** Window duration and gaze counts are therefore mechanically linked to skip versus charge, which is why the redefined target conditions on a charge.
- **The assistant was not logged.** The in-game assistant replies and the benign or malicious condition are not in the data, so their association with decisions cannot be assessed.
- **Several grids chose their weakest setting**; a wider ridge grid gave the same conclusion, but tuning remains a limited part of the picture.
- **Language-model scenario set.** Quota-sampled, text-only, one main decoding setting, image descriptions written by another model; the investigators were not blind to the labels. The results do not transfer to a deployed assistant or to human behaviour.
- **Multiplicity.** Many strata and sensitivity runs were made; unadjusted p-values are descriptive. Where a Holm bound or an FDR value is given, it covers only the family named.

## 7. Recommended design for new data collection

The existing data cannot answer whether gaze, EEG or an assistant changes risky decisions. A new study could:

1. Pre-register the scenario set, the primary outcome, the exclusion rules and the analysis, and keep a held-out set of participants untouched until the analysis is fixed.
2. Randomise three assistant conditions (no assistant, correct recommendation, malicious recommendation) and **log the assistant replies and the condition for every decision**.
3. Log head pose and the world positions of the kiosks, so gaze can be mapped to areas of interest without video.
4. Record decision latency and pre-decision gaze as defined measures with the decision window fixed before the outcome is known.
5. Record EEG for the whole session with start and end markers, check signal quality online, and report the usable sample before analysis.
6. Counterbalance trial order and kiosk layout so exposure is not confounded with position, time or collection period.
7. Use a sample size justified by the smallest difference of interest rather than by availability.
8. Evaluate any language-model assistant with people, not only with scripted prompts [CITE: automation bias in decision support].
